## "It Depends" Is a Real Answer. Here's What It Depends On.

Before the numbers, it helps to know what actually moves the price up or down. Every website quote you get, from anyone, is really a reflection of these variables:

- **Number of pages.** A 5-page brochure site costs less than a 25-page site with service-specific landing pages for every neighborhood you cover.
- **Custom functionality.** A contact form is cheap. Online booking, a customer portal, inventory-synced e-commerce, or a staff scheduling system all add real development time.
- **Design: template vs. custom.** A themed template you can buy for $60 is not the same product as a layout built around your brand and your actual customer journey.
- **Who writes the content.** If you're handing over finished copy and photos, that's less work than a developer interviewing you and writing everything from scratch.
- **SEO scope.** A site that just exists is different from a site built with schema markup, clean URLs, and location pages meant to rank.
- **Timeline.** Rush jobs cost more everywhere, not just in web design.
- **What happens after launch.** Is maintenance included? Is there a monthly fee? Who fixes it when something breaks?

Every quote you'll ever get is some combination of these seven things. Once you can see a quote through that lens, "it depends" stops being a dodge and starts being useful.

## The Four Ways Houston Businesses Actually Get a Website Built

### 1. DIY on Wix, Squarespace, or GoDaddy

Advertised at $16 to $40 a month. By the time you add a custom domain, remove platform branding, buy a premium template, and add the apps or plugins you actually need, a realistic first-year cost lands somewhere between $300 and $900, plus 30 to 60 hours of your own time learning the platform, writing content, and fixing mobile display issues.

That time cost is real. If your time is worth even $50 an hour, a DIY build that takes 40 hours has an actual cost closer to $2,000 once you're honest about it, even though the platform itself was "cheap."

### 2. A Freelancer

Marketplace or referral freelancers in the Houston market typically run $1,500 to $5,000 for a small business site. Quality is the wild card here: some freelancers are excellent and some disappear mid-project. You're also usually the only person managing the relationship, so scope changes and revisions depend entirely on how that one person runs their business.

### 3. A Traditional Agency

Full-service agencies in Houston commonly quote $5,000 to $25,000 and up, often with a monthly retainer layered on top for "ongoing management." You're paying for account managers, a design team, and overhead that a one-person or small shop doesn't carry. That can be worth it for a large company with a marketing department to coordinate with. For a five-person service business, it's usually more structure than you need.

### 4. A Flat-Rate Specialist

This is the model StephensCode runs on: you pick a tier, the price is fixed before any work starts, and there's no surprise change order halfway through. It exists specifically to close the gap between "cheap and generic" and "expensive and overbuilt" for small and mid-sized businesses.

## The Costs That Show Up After Launch

The purchase price, whichever route you choose, isn't the whole story. Almost every website carries some ongoing cost, and it's worth knowing what's normal so nobody surprises you with it later:

- **Hosting.** Typically $10-30/month for a small business site, regardless of who built it.
- **Domain renewal.** Usually $12-20/year, easy to forget until it lapses.
- **SSL certificate.** Most modern hosting includes this at no extra charge, but confirm it.
- **Content updates.** Changing a phone number or adding a new service page shouldn't require a full redesign. Ask upfront what a simple update costs and how fast it typically turns around.
- **SEO maintenance.** A site that ranks well on launch day can slip over time if nobody's watching it. This is optional, not mandatory, but it's worth knowing whether it's included or sold separately.

None of these should add up to a second mortgage. If a "low-cost" website comes with $150/month in required add-ons to actually function, that's not really a low-cost website, it's a subscription with a small down payment.

## What Houston Business Owners Get Wrong About Timing

A common mistake is waiting until the business is "big enough" to justify a real website, and running on a $250 placeholder indefinitely. The better way to think about it: match the tier to what you need to close business *now*, not to some future version of the company.

A landscaping crew with three trucks doesn't need a $5,000 client portal. A growing multi-location clinic juggling appointment volume across sites probably does. The tiers exist precisely so you're not paying enterprise prices for a five-page brochure site, or trying to run a real operation off a template that was never built to handle it.

## Questions Worth Asking Before You Pay Anyone for a Website

Regardless of who you hire, ask these before signing anything:

1. What's actually included at this price, and what counts as a change order?
2. Do I own the code and design files, or am I locked into your platform?
3. Are there ongoing monthly fees beyond hosting, and what do they cover?
4. What happens if I want a different developer to take over later?
5. Is there a fixed number of revisions, and what happens after that?
6. What's the realistic timeline, in writing?

If a quote can't answer these clearly, that's information too.
