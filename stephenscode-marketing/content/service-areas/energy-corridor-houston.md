## Two Customer Bases, One Website Trying to Serve Both

Here is the thing that makes the Energy Corridor genuinely unusual as a market, and it is not something I say about many places I write about: the customer base here splits almost cleanly in two, and a lot of local businesses are trying to serve both halves with a website that was never built to do that.

On one side, you have the weekday corporate crowd. Tens of thousands of professionals moving through ConocoPhillips, BP, Shell, and the surrounding office towers and satellite offices that orbit those headquarters. These people need B2B services during business hours -- IT support, catering, printing and signage, commercial cleaning, staffing, equipment rental, engineering subcontractors, professional consulting. Their buying decisions often run through a procurement process, or at minimum through a coworker who got asked to "find someone who can handle this" and went straight to Google.

On the other side, you have the residential population -- the families in Nottingham Forest, Briar Forest, the Eldridge area, and Park Row who live here specifically because of the Energy Corridor's mix of newer housing stock, proximity to major employers, and access to Terry Hershey Park. On evenings and weekends, this is a completely different customer: someone looking for a pediatric dentist, a landscaper, a birthday party venue, a home remodeler, or a good place to grab dinner that is not a chain restaurant off the freeway.

A single website that tries to speak generically to "everyone" usually ends up speaking clearly to no one. If you are a commercial services company, your site needs to read as credible to a corporate buyer scanning it in ninety seconds between meetings. If you are a restaurant or a family-facing service, your site needs to read as inviting and local to someone deciding where to spend a Saturday afternoon. I have built both kinds of sites, and I build them differently on purpose, because the person reading them is different.

## Why a Website Matters More in a B2B-Heavy Market

Most of the towns I write about have a straightforward problem: businesses do not have much of a web presence at all, and getting a decent site online instantly puts them ahead of the pack. The Energy Corridor is a more complicated case, because a real share of the businesses here are selling to other businesses, not directly to consumers, and B2B buying behavior works differently than consumer buying behavior.

When a facilities manager at an energy company or a project lead at an engineering firm needs a vendor, they do not scroll Facebook. They search, they open two or three websites in tabs, and they form a judgment about competence in the first thirty seconds based almost entirely on how professional and specific those sites look. A vague homepage with stock photography and a paragraph of marketing filler tells that buyer nothing about whether you can actually do the work. A site that clearly states your services, shows your credentials or certifications, names the kind of clients you serve, and makes it simple to request a quote or schedule a call tells them you already operate like a business worth trusting with a contract.

This matters just as much for the consulting firms, engineering shops, and technology companies that have set up shop near the corporate campuses specifically to be close to their clients. If your own client base is sophisticated -- energy companies, their contractors, their vendors -- your website is one of the few places you get to make a first impression before a relationship starts, and it needs to hold up to the same scrutiny you would apply to one of theirs.
